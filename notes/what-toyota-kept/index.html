What Toyota kept

Every conversation about handing work to a machine reaches the same moment. The owner leans back a bit and says some version of: so what you are telling me is I lose control of my own business.

It is a completely reasonable thing to be worried about. It is also the wrong worry, and the cleanest counter-example has been running since the 1950s.

Toyota automated the line and then put a cord next to every person standing at it.

Anyone on that line can pull it and stop production. Not a supervisor. Anyone. The idea has a name inside the company, and the translation people usually give it is automation with a human element. The reason it exists is boring and correct: a machine is extraordinary at doing the identical thing ten thousand times and completely useless at noticing that this particular one came out wrong. A person is the exact opposite. They are bad at the ten thousand and very good at the one.

So the machine runs the repetition, and the person keeps the authority. Nobody at Toyota confused those two jobs, which is why they were still building the good cars while everyone else was arguing about whether robots were coming for the line.

What that means for a twelve-person company

The same split, scaled down. There are things in your business that happen the same way every single time, and the only reason they ever fail is that a human being was busy, tired, or on a job. Invoices going out. Quotes getting chased. The reminder before the appointment. The review request after the work. None of those need judgment. They need someone to not forget, which is the one thing people are unreliable at and machines are unreasonably good at.

Then there is everything else. Whether this customer is worth bending the rules for. Whether that job smells wrong. Whether the guy on the phone is upset about the price or about being ignored for three days. That is your cord. Nothing we build gets to touch it.

The test we use

Before we build anything we ask one question about it: if this does the wrong thing on a Tuesday, how fast do you find out and how fast can you stop it?

If the answer is "quickly, and you turn it off yourself", it is a good candidate. If the answer is "you would find out in a month from an angry customer", we do not build it, or we build a smaller version that tells you what it is about to do before it does it.

You should be able to read every message that goes out before it goes out, for as long as you want to. Most people watch closely for two weeks and then stop, which is fine. What matters is that stopping was your choice.

The version of this that people are actually afraid of does exist. It is the setup where something runs your customer relationships, nobody can explain what it decided or why, and the vendor treats the black box as a feature. That is a real thing and it is worth being suspicious of.

It is just not the only option, and it never was. Toyota worked that out seventy years ago and kept the cord.
