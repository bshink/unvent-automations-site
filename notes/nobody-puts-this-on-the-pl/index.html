Nobody puts this on the P&L

If a truck breaks, you know exactly what it cost. There is an invoice. Somebody is annoyed about it. It goes in a column, and next quarter you can point at it.

The losses that actually add up do not work like that. They look like a normal week.

  • A quote went out on the ninth. It was good work, correctly priced. Nobody followed up because Thursday happened. They went with someone who called them back.
  • Three calls came in between 11:40 and 11:55 while everybody was heads-down. Two left voicemails. One did not, and you will never know they existed.
  • An invoice sat in someone's drafts for nine days. You still got paid. You got paid nine days later than you should have, and that happened forty times this year.
  • Six hundred past customers have not heard a word from you in two years. You paid to acquire every single one of them.
None of that has a line item, so nobody fixes it, and it keeps happening every week for years.

Why it survives

Because each individual instance is genuinely small, and because it is nobody's fault. There is no bad decision to point at. Everyone involved was doing something reasonable. The quote did not get chased because chasing quotes is the fourth most important thing anybody had on Thursday, and it will be the fourth most important thing next Thursday too.

That is also exactly why it does not get better on its own, and why hiring somebody usually does not fix it either. You have now paid a salary for the fourth-most-important thing to have an owner, and it is still the fourth most important thing.

What actually moves it

Making it not depend on anyone deciding to do it. The quote follow-up goes out because the quote went out, not because a person had a spare ten minutes. The invoice goes out the day the work is finished. The reminder goes out because the appointment exists.

The unglamorous truth is that most of what we build is not clever. It is a small number of things that always happen, happening always. The clever part is only ever working out which four of them are costing you the most, which is the entire content of the first call.

There is a rough version of that arithmetic on the front page. It is deliberately conservative, it does not ask for your email, and the maths runs on your own screen. It will not be exactly right about you. It is usually low.

Or skip it and just answer this one honestly: what is the thing that falls through every few weeks that everyone has quietly agreed to live with? That is the one. It always is.
